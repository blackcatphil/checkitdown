How to play poker in a Vegas room

Nobody explains any of this at the desk. Here is what actually happens between walking in and getting dealt a hand — and the handful of things that mark you out as new.

Getting on the list

Walk to the podium — the desk at the front of the room — and say the game you want. “One-three, please.” That's it. They'll ask for initials and a first name, not ID, and your name goes on a screen you can read from the rail.

If there's a wait, ask two questions: how many are ahead of you, and whether they'll text you. Most rooms will. Then go and do something else — nobody stands at the podium for forty minutes. Some rooms let you join the list from your phone before you arrive; the rooms that do are marked in the list.

WORTH KNOWING Being on two lists at two rooms is normal and nobody minds. Take the first seat that opens and call the other room to drop off.

Buying chips

You don't need chips before you sit. Take the seat, put your cash on the table in front of you, and tell the dealer how much you're playing. A chip runner brings your stack in a minute or two and you can play with cash in the meantime — cash plays, in Nevada, at the amount you announce.

Buy in for more than the minimum if you can. A $100 stack in a $1/3 game leaves you no room to play after one raise, and every stack at the table will have you covered. The buy-in range for every room and stake is on each room's page.

Tipping, honestly

Dealers are tipped out of pots you win — roughly a dollar a hand, a little more on a big one, nothing on a pot you take without a flop. It isn't optional in practice, and it comes out of the rake-paying economy you're already in, so factor it in when you work out what a session costs.

Drinks are free while you're playing. The server will find you; tip a dollar or two a drink and she'll find you faster.

Sitting down mid-game

You'll be sent to a table that's already running. Take the empty seat, hand your money forward, and wait for the blind to pass you — or post it now if you'd rather play immediately. Either is fine; say which.

Ask the dealer anything. What's the straddle rule here, is there a must-post, where's the button. Dealers answer this fifty times a shift and would rather you asked than guessed. Room-by-room, we list straddle and must-post rules so you can read them before you sit.

None of it is about skill. Angle-free, in turn, chips visible, phone down when you're in a hand — do that and nobody at the table can tell how long you've been playing.

What it costs to sit there

The house takes a cut of each pot — the rake — usually 10% capped at four or five dollars, sometimes with a dollar or two on top for the jackpot. Spread across a full table that's real money per hour, and it varies by room more than most players realise.

Comps run the other way: most rooms credit you a couple of dollars an hour toward food. We price every room and stake side by side, with the arithmetic shown, because a $4 cap with no jackpot drop is a meaningfully cheaper seat than a $5 cap with two.
